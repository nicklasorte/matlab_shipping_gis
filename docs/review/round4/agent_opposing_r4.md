# Round-four opposing review: commercial and federal rebuttals

Date: November 14, 2026. Reviewer: blind opposing reader, playing two filers. One is a commercial wireless party that wants the zones as small as possible. The other is a federal or maritime party that wants them larger to protect ship radars. Paper: `paper_rev486.md` (doc revision 486; 34 pending style edits not included). Closed items from `../agent_opposing.md`, `../agent_opposing2.md`, `../round2/agent_opposing_r2.md`, `../round2/findings_r2.md`, `../round3/findings_r3.md`, and `../mock_rebuttal_rev1.md` aren't repeated unless the paper still leaves them open.

## Summary

This review lists 17 rebuttals: 8 from the commercial side (smaller zones) and 9 from the federal side (larger zones). Three are **Fix**, 11 are **Should fix**, and 3 are **Note**. The paper fully answers 1, partly answers 10, and doesn't answer 6. Four of the strongest attacks are new and rest on numbers already in the repo. First, the zones depend on how many days were sampled. Half-samples give 1% to 5% less land than the full 104 days, so a full-year build would likely give more land (R4OPP-9). Second, the paper calls fishing vessels "non-SOLAS," but SOLAS Chapter V applies to them, and the ship-type filter drops every fishing vessel whatever its size (R4OPP-10). Third, the tug-barge rule adds 12% of the 25 km land, but no carriage rule puts an S-band radar on a tug (R4OPP-1). Fourth, the Great Lakes routes alone account for about a quarter of the lower-48 zone land, and that land rests on a carriage assumption the paper itself calls unsupported (R4OPP-2). The paper also calls its AIS source terrestrial. Yet 52% of the lower-48 route cells lie more than 50 nautical miles from the coast, which undercuts the Alaska receiver-reach reasoning (R4OPP-12). The route counting, port checks, threshold sweep, and boundary caveats hold up, and neither filer would get far attacking them.

## Reviewer calculations

R4OPP-2, R4OPP-1 (check), and R4OPP-7 use the same quick method: AIS route cells from `outputs/2024_2025/viz/viz_cells.npz` (658,867 cells, no charted lanes or racons), the 0.05° land points in `geo_land_distance.npz` weighted by cos(latitude), and great-circle nearest-cell distance on a 6,371.0088 km sphere. The method reproduces the file's distances (correlation 0.9999998, median difference 0.08 km). It also reproduces the published no-tug-barge change to within about 1 point (−11.2%, −5.3%, and −2.0% against −12.0%, −6.0%, and −2.3%). The Great Lakes and both-years numbers are estimates on that basis, not polygon areas.

## What holds up

- Route counting, the filter funnel, and the two-implementation check (Tables 1 to 3). Neither filer gains much there.
- The threshold sweep (3 to 8 ships within about ±6%) and the port-approach, flagged-report, and length sensitivity cases.
- The equidistance caveats (Gulf of Maine, the 1990 line, unresolved areas) and the 200-nautical-mile cap.
- The Alaska cutoff search. `satellite_cutoff_sweep_rev1.json` matches 5.3 (0.571 at 272, 0.573 at 300, 0.453 at 650).
- ITU-R M.1460 (radiodetermination radars in 2900–3100 MHz, shipborne maritime radionavigation radars included) sets a protection criterion of I/N = −6 dB "even if multiple interferers are present." Section 2 can cite it when it sets the distances. Until then, the paper's position that every distance is an example is the right defense against both filers.
